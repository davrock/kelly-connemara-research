1. The name and what it means

Kelly is the anglicised form of the Gaelic Ó Ceallaigh — “descendant of Ceallach.” It is one of the oldest and most widespread surnames in Ireland. In English it also appears as O’Kelly, Kelley and Kellie, all from the one Irish original.

Ó Ceallaigh Ua Ceallaigh O’Kelly Kelly Kelley Kellie

The personal name Ceallach is of genuinely disputed meaning. It has traditionally been read as “bright-headed,” but scholars have also linked it to ceall (“church”), giving the sense “frequenting churches,” while an older strand connects it to words for “war,” “strife” or “contention.” Library Ireland, following MacLysaght, glosses the root as “descendant of Ceallach (war, contention).” All three senses are best treated as candidates rather than a settled answer (Library Ireland; Wikipedia, “Kelly (surname)”).

2. Kelly is not one family

The single most important fact for anyone researching the name: “Kelly” does not descend from one ancestor or one clan. The surname arose independently among many unrelated families across Ireland — which is exactly why it became so numerous. Genealogists count as many as ten distinct Ó Ceallaigh septs, each with its own territory (Library Ireland).

What this means for a family tree. A modern Kelly cannot assume descent from any one sept — least of all the famous Uí Maine kings — on the strength of the surname alone. Only documentary genealogy or DNA evidence can show which stock a given family belongs to. That is why the private tree on this site is built strictly from records, not from the surname’s fame.

3. The O’Kellys of Uí Maine (Hy-Many)

The greatest of the Kelly septs was the ruling dynasty of Uí Maine (Hy-Many), a Gaelic kingdom of south Connacht that took in all of Co. Galway east of Athenry together with southern and central Co. Roscommon — territory long known as “O’Kelly’s Country.” The kingdom was named for its semi-legendary founder, Máine Mór (Wikipedia, “Kings of Uí Maine”).

The surname itself traces to Cellach mac Fíonachta in the 9th century; the first man actually to bear it, his grandson Tadhg Mór Ó Ceallaigh, was slain at the Battle of Clontarf in 1014 fighting for his ally Brian Boru. As chiefs of Uí Maine the O’Kellys “were one of the most powerful families in Connacht… which they held down to the reign of Elizabeth,” their chief styled simply “The O’Kelly” (Wikipedia, “Ó Ceallaigh”; Library Ireland).

“All the poetic classes in Ireland” were invited to the O’Kelly’s Christmas feast of 1351 — remembered ever after as the welcome of the O’Kellys.

The lordship reached its late-medieval height under William Buide (Buí) Ó Ceallaigh, king of Uí Maine c.1349–1381. At Christmas 1351 he hosted a famous gathering of the learned and poetic classes of Ireland, an event long remembered as a byword for Gaelic hospitality, and in 1353 he founded Kilconnell Friary (History Ireland). The dynasty’s prestige is embodied in the Book of Uí Maine (Leabhar Ua Maine), a great vellum manuscript compiled c.1392–94 for Muircheartach Ó Ceallaigh (d. 1407), Bishop of Clonfert and Archbishop of Tuam, containing genealogies, poems and pedigrees (Wikipedia, “Leabhar Ua Maine”; Oxford Reference).

The O’Kellys studded their country with castles — among them Aughrim, Monivea, Gallagh, Mullaghmore, Moylough and Garbally in Galway, and Galey, Athleague and Skryne in Roscommon (Library Ireland, O’Kelly pedigree).

4. Arms, the enfield & the motto

The arms usually blazoned for the O’Kellys of Uí Maine show, on a blue (azure) field, a silver tower triple-towered between two silver lions. The famous element, though, is the crest: an enfield — a mythical beast said to combine the head of a fox, the forequarters of a greyhound, the body of a lion, the hindquarters of a wolf and the talons of an eagle. By clan tradition it rose from the sea to guard the body of Tadhg Mór at Clontarf. The family motto is “Turris fortis mihi Deus” — “God is a strong tower to me.”

5. The Kellys of Connemara

The Uí Maine heartland lay inland, in east Galway and Roscommon. The Kellys of Iar-Chonnacht (West Connacht) and Connemara — the Atlantic coastal parishes of west Co. Galway — sit in a different world. Connemara was historically part of the lordship of Iar Connacht and today holds the largest Irish-speaking Gaeltacht in Ireland, stretching from Bearna and An Spidéal westward through Ros Muc and the Leitir Móir / Garmna / Leitir Mealláin (Lettermore, Gorumna, Lettermullan) islands of Kilkieran Bay to Carna and Cill Chiaráin (Wikipedia, “Connemara”; Wikipedia, “Gaeltacht”).

Coastal families such as the Kellys around Lettermullen, Mace and Carna belong to this Irish-speaking, seaboard world rather than to the inland Ó Ceallaigh chieftainry. It was a dense, Gaelic-speaking population of small farmers and fishermen, with strong traditions of storytelling, music and weaving — and one of the poorest and most “congested” districts of the west (Wikipedia, “Connemara”).

Our line. The family documented in the private tree on this site is a Connemara Kelly line — from the townlands around Glan, Mace and Lettermullen — not a branch of the Uí Maine kings. Its story is one of Irish-speaking Atlantic Ireland and, later, emigration. Everything in the tree is built from parish registers, civil records and census returns rather than from the surname’s medieval fame.

6. Leaving: Connemara to America

The Great Famine and the evictions and hardship that followed drove heavy emigration out of the west of Ireland. From the Connemara Gaeltacht that emigration often followed chain-migration paths — whole families and townlands funnelling toward the same American destinations, where earlier arrivals housed and found work for those who came after.

For the Carna and south-Connemara districts, one of the best-documented destinations was Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania — a link maintained well into the twentieth century (Irish America, on the Carna emigrants). The emigrant children of the Connemara Kellys in the private tree appear as labelled leaves at the point they left, marking where the branches of the family went.

To research a Kelly line of your own

Start with what a record can prove, not with the surname. The Irish civil registers and the 1901 and 1911 censuses are free online at irishgenealogy.ie and census.nationalarchives.ie; Griffith’s Valuation and church registers help push earlier. Because “Kelly” is so common, place — the townland and parish — matters more than the name in telling one family from another.
