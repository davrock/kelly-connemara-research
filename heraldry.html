Heraldry of the O’Kellys

The arms, the enfield crest and the motto of the O’Kellys of Uí Maine — the heritage of the name, with an honest note on who a coat of arms actually belongs to.

Whose arms are these? In Ireland a coat of arms belongs to a particular lineage — here the chiefly O’Kelly line of Uí Maine — not to everyone who happens to share the surname. They are shown here as part of the heritage of the name, which every Kelly can enjoy, and not as evidence that any one family descends from the medieval kings.

The shield

Azure, a tower triple-towered argent

A silver, three-turreted tower on a blue field — in the fuller arms flanked by two silver lions. The tower gives the motto its meaning.

After the O’Kelly of Uí Maine arms. This is an artistic rendering of the blazon, not a scan of a specific grant.

The enfield crest

An enfield vert

A mythical beast said to join the head of a fox, the forequarters of a greyhound, the body of a lion, the hindquarters of a wolf and the talons of an eagle. The Irish call it the onchú.

Artistic rendering of the heraldic enfield, after the O’Kelly crest.

The motto

Turris fortis mihi Deus

“God is a strong tower to me” — a line that echoes the triple-towered charge on the shield.

Standard O’Kelly motto.

The legend of the enfield

By clan tradition — and this is legend, not documented history — when Tadhg Mór Ó Ceallaigh fell at the Battle of Clontarf in 1014, an enfield rose out of the sea and stood guard over his body, keeping it from harm until his kinsmen could recover it for proper burial. In gratitude the family took the beast as their crest, and it has appeared on O’Kelly arms ever since (tradition, as related in heraldic collections; see the enfield entry).

Whatever its origin, the enfield is one of the most distinctive crests in Irish heraldry — a creature found almost nowhere else — and it has become a badge of the O’Kelly name.
